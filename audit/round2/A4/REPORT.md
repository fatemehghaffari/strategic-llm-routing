### 2b. Continuous contexts

`x` is the query text embedded with TF-IDF over word 1-2 grams and reduced by truncated SVD
(latent semantic analysis), then rank-transformed coordinate by coordinate so that each
coordinate is uniform on [0,1], which is the context space `[0,1]^d` the paper's continuous
results assume. It is a local CPU embedding; no paid API and no external service was called.
`M_i(x)` is model `i`'s mean published score over the `k` nearest OTHER queries to `x`, a
leave-one-out local average.

**Why d = 2.** The mechanism partitions `[0,1]^D` into `b_n^D` cells. In LLM routing one query
arrives and every provider sees the same query, so the profile lies on the diagonal of
`[0,1]^{Kd}` and the number of cells carrying positive mass is `b_n^d`, not `b_n^{Kd}`; the
paper's own remark about lazily initialised cells is what makes that legitimate. With
`h_n = n^{-1/(D+3)}` and `n = 300,000` this gives b = 13 and **129 populated cells** at d = 2,
which is the largest d at which every populated cell still receives enough visits at our horizon.
d = 1 (24 cells) and d = 3 (415 cells, minimum cell size 1) are reported alongside; k = 200 is
primary with k = 50 and k = 800 alongside. Results are stable across all nine (d, k) settings.

**Diagnostic: the embedding is not noise.** At d = 2 the grid cells have 0.891 purity with
respect to the benchmark family and normalised mutual information 0.775 with it. The TF-IDF
cells recover most of the family structure without ever being told it.

**Flips on the continuous space**, d = 2, k = 200: the cell-level pairwise flip rate is
**0.2070** over 454,080 cell-pair comparisons; four distinct models are pointwise best in some
cell; and on **20.4 per cent of queries** the pointwise-best model is not the globally best
model. Mean pointwise-max capability 0.820 against global-max 0.817; mean pointwise-second 0.763
against global-second 0.734.

The pointwise-max figures say something important and unflattering: with all eleven models in
play, knowing the context buys almost nothing at the TOP (0.820 against 0.817) because GPT-4
dominates. It buys 0.029 at the SECOND capability, which is the benchmark the mechanism actually
delivers. The contextual advantage in this data lives at the second capability, not at the first.

## 2c. THE TWO-TOP CONDITION, MEASURED

This is the quantitative finding I would put first if the paper wanted one result from this
package. The condition that at least two providers attain the largest capability is the hinge of
the paper: it makes the public contextual equilibrium work (Theorems `thm:grid-ucb-robust` and
`thm:contextual-public`), it is what rules out the low-revenue equilibria of Theorem
`thm:equilibrium-set`, and it is what governs pairwise collusion. It is an exact-tie condition, so
the useful question is quantitative: at tolerance `delta`, on how much ARRIVAL MASS are the top
two capabilities within `delta`?

Computed for every subset of the eleven models of every size from 2 to 11, on both context sets,
with the empirical arrival masses. Source: `experiments/data/A4/two_top_condition.csv`,
`two_top_summary.csv`, `two_top_best_subsets.csv`.

**Exactly: never.** The mass on which the top two capabilities are exactly equal is **0.0000** for
every one of the 2,036 subsets, at every size, on both context sets. Measured capabilities are
real numbers and never tie.

|---|---|---|---|

Every subset that achieves an approximate two-top condition on the whole arrival mass at tolerance
0.05 excludes GPT-4, and adding GPT-4 is what breaks it: the mass within 0.05 falls from 0.675 to
0.557 and the mean gap rises from 0.035 to 0.056.

**What this means for the paper, stated as a claim it can make.** The two-top condition should not
be presented as a mild regularity assumption. On measured LLM capabilities it never holds exactly,
and whether it holds approximately is decided by one thing: whether the market contains a provider
that is clear of the field. On the sub-market of comparable providers it holds at tolerance 0.05 on
the entire arrival mass, and there the paper's good branch applies. With the dominant provider in
the market it holds on about half the mass at tolerance 0.05, and the operative guarantee is the
honest benchmark. That is a sharp, testable and useful statement, and it is also exactly where the
undercut deviation of section 4.4 is most profitable, since a uniquely top provider is precisely
one with room to undercut. The two halves of this package agree.

**What the paper is missing and should add.** There is no result anywhere in it about an
APPROXIMATE two-top condition. The natural conjecture, which I did not prove and did not test, is
that a top-to-second gap of `g` on mass `m` degrades the equilibrium revenue by something of order
`g` on that mass rather than collapsing it, in which case 0.056 would be a tolerable loss and the
paper's headline would survive contact with real capabilities. As things stand the theory is
all-or-nothing and the data always falls on the wrong side of it.

## 3. Step 3: the assumption I cannot measure

RouterBench records what each model achieved at the configuration it was run at. That is the
capability `M_i`. It records nothing at all about the intrinsic level `mu_i`, meaning what a
provider would deliver if it spent the minimum. **`mu_i` is therefore assumed, not measured, and
no experiment on this data can avoid assuming it.**

The assumptions used, all of the form `mu_i(x) = alpha_i * M_i(x)`:

| tag | alpha |
|---|---|
| `a0.00` | 0 for every provider: a provider that delivers nothing at zero effort |
| `a0.25` | 0.25 for every provider |
| `a0.50` | 0.5 for every provider |
| `a0.75` | 0.75 for every provider: effort buys little |
| `het` | 0.2, 0.5, 0.8 assigned to the providers in DESCENDING order of context-averaged capability, so the strongest provider is the one with the least slack |

Every headline result is reported for all five. Where a conclusion holds only for some alpha,
the report says which. The honest benchmark is `E[max over honest providers of mu_h(x)]`, so it
is identically zero at `alpha = 0` and there is nothing to report against it there.

The honest set is a design choice too, and it is: **exactly one honest provider, the one with the
lowest context-averaged capability**, which is the least favourable nonempty honest set for the
robustness guarantee. Everyone else may lower its target below its intrinsic level.

## 4. Step 4: the headline comparison

### 4.0 Design

Every comparison is run TWICE.

* **NON-STRATEGIC world.** Every provider always delivers its measured capability `M_i(x)`, in
  every phase, whatever the mechanism asks. This is the world the routing literature's rules are
  designed for.
* **STRATEGIC world.** Every provider chooses the option that maximises its own utility
  `u_i = T_i - C_i`, found by best-response iteration from the non-strategic profile. The declared
  option set per provider is `theta in {0, .25, .5, .75, 1}` (deliver
  `mu_i + theta (M_i - mu_i)` pointwise), `proposed` (the paper's `S*`), `selective` (deliver
  capability where pointwise top, the intrinsic level elsewhere) and `boundary` (elicit at
  capability, then track the monitoring boundary from below). Section 4.4 repeats the exercise
  with a much finer option set, and that turns out to matter a great deal.

Rules compared: PL-MESP or CG-MESP (contextual), MESP (scalar, one number elicited per provider),
and a quality-estimating router with greedy, epsilon-greedy and UCB exploration. The oracle
(pointwise best) and the honest benchmark are computed in closed form on the same arrival path.
`n = 300,000`, 10 seeds per profile, three model triples stratified by measured flip rate, five
assumptions about `mu`, three finite context sets and the continuous one.

**Labelling, which is not negotiable.** The router is a POLICY CLASS: per-context empirical means
from observed outcomes plus one of three standard exploration rules. It stands for the line of
work that routes on estimated quality (FrugalGPT, RouteLLM, Hybrid LLM, Zooter, AutoMix, and
RouterBench's own routers). **I did not train, run, tune or reproduce any of those systems, and no
number here is a benchmark of any of them.**

### 4.1 Contextual against scalar, and the claim that fails

The claim to test was: the scalar mechanism loses to the contextual one by an amount tracking the
flip rate. Measured on revenue, **it does not lose.**

| horizon, all 165 three-model subsets, `alpha = 0.5`, Q = 3 | value |
|---|---|
| mean contextual minus scalar revenue per round, `n = 300,000` | **+0.0084** |
| subsets where that is positive, `n = 300,000` | 71.5 per cent |
| Spearman of that gap with the measured flip rate, `n = 300,000` | 0.134 (p = 0.086) |
| mean contextual minus scalar revenue per round, `n = 3,000,000` | **-0.0239** |
| subsets where that is positive, `n = 3,000,000` | 47.3 per cent |
| Spearman of that gap with the measured flip rate, `n = 3,000,000` | 0.055 (p = 0.48) |

Analytically, with the sublinear overheads removed, the gap is real and it does track the flip
rate: the contextual mechanism delivers `E[M_(2)(x)]`, the scalar one delivers
`E[min(tau, M_W(x))]` with one global price, and the difference averages 0.0227 (Q = 3) and
0.0236 (Q = 7), reaching 0.072, with Spearman 0.565 and 0.437 against the flip rate (both
p below 1e-10) and slope 0.052 to 0.055 per unit flip rate. So the mechanism-design quantity
behaves exactly as the paper predicts, and the simulated revenue does not reproduce it.

**Why, precisely.** The scalar price is a global average and is not feasible at the winner
pointwise: it exceeds the winner's own capability on 19.6 per cent of arrival mass on average and
somewhere in 77.6 per cent of subsets. The monitor then does its job and ejects a FULLY COMPLIANT
winner. At `n = 3,000,000` this happens in 0.537 runs on average for the scalar mechanism and in
**0.000** for the contextual one, and the rate rises with the flip rate (Spearman 0.278,
p = 3e-4; flip-rate quartile means 0.444, 0.520, 0.913). But ejection restarts EXP3 over a set of
providers who are all delivering their capability, so the fallback earns close to the POINTWISE
MAXIMUM, which is above the second capability the theorem promises. The scalar mechanism is paid
for its own failure. The horizon sweep shows it happening: on one Q = 7 triple the scalar
mechanism earns 0.6795 at `n = 10^7` before it starts being ejected and 0.7918 once ejection is
certain, while the contextual mechanism sits at 0.6795 throughout.

**Conclusion, stated plainly.** On this data, revenue at the proposed profile does not
discriminate between a contextual and a scalar mechanism, and at large horizons it favours the
scalar one for the wrong reason. The axis that discriminates, and that behaves as the paper
predicts, is whether the announced price is feasible at the realised winner. That is also the
hypothesis the whole continuum argument runs on, so it is the right axis, but the paper should
not claim a revenue advantage it cannot show.

### 4.4 The deviation the coarse grid hid, and the one result that goes the paper's way

With the coarse option set the quality-estimating routers showed **no degradation at all**: the
best response of every provider was to deliver its capability and the best single deviation gained
0.0000. That is not because a router is incentive compatible. It is because the coarse grid's
smallest shading step, `theta = 0.75`, is far too large to stay pointwise best, and in this utility
model a provider that loses its selections loses everything (section 6g).

The exact best response against a rule that routes to the estimated best provider is not a
constant fraction. It is to UNDERCUT: deliver
`m_i(x) = max(mu_i(x), min(M_i(x), M_(2)(x) + eps))`, staying pointwise best by `eps` and banking
`M_i(x) - M_(2)(x) - eps` on every selection. That is precisely the deviation the paper's two-top
condition exists to rule out. I added it, plus a fine theta grid at 0.90, 0.95, 0.975 and 0.99,
and reran one deviator at a time against every rule, on four triples (the three stratified ones
plus `GPT-4 | Yi-34B | Mistral-7B`, chosen because GPT-4 is uniquely top on most contexts, which
is the case the condition is about), five assumptions about `mu`, 10 seeds, `n = 300,000`.

**This is the result that goes the paper's way, and it is large.** Best gain found by a single
deviator, per round, over 16 cells per rule:

| router, epsilon-greedy | 0.0422 | **0.0648** | **0.0648** | 0.0516 | 0.0983 |
| router, UCB | 0.0272 | **0.0578** | **0.0578** | 0.0513 | 0.0717 |

Undercutting is the winning deviation against the epsilon-greedy and UCB routers in **20 of 20**

does not.**

Analytically the ceiling on this deviation is `E[M_(1)(x) - M_(2)(x)]`, which averages 0.108 per
round over the 165 subsets and reaches 0.299. The simulated numbers are smaller because at
`n = 300,000` the routers still explore, the `eps` grid is coarse, and the deviator is not
uniquely top on every context of these particular triples. The analytic figure is the right one
to quote as the size of the exposure; the simulated one is what a single stationary deviator
actually collected at this horizon.

**How to read sections 4.2 and 4.4 together, which is the honest summary of this package.** There
are two different vulnerabilities and each rule has one of them.

* A quality-estimating router is safe against uniform shading and exposed to undercutting: gain up
  to 0.065 per round, platform loss up to 0.098.
* The monitored second price is safe against undercutting, by construction, and exposed to the
  LOSER'S indifference: the providers who cannot win shade their elicited values, the price is the
  second elicited value, and revenue falls to the honest benchmark. In equilibrium that costs more
  than the router's exposure on this data.

The paper's own theory covers both: the first is the two-top condition, the second is Theorem
`thm:equilibrium-set`. What the data adds is that on measured LLM capabilities the two-top
condition never holds (section 6f), so both branches bite at once, and which rule a platform
should prefer depends on whether it wants a guaranteed floor or a good expectation.

### 4.5 Utility against effort, per arm role

Nothing else in this package shows the mechanism from the AGENT's side, which is where incentive
compatibility actually lives. For a fixed instance and a fixed single context, with every other
provider held at the proposed profile, one provider's DELIVERED MEAN is swept across its whole
feasible range on a 61-point grid and its OWN utility per round is recorded, once per rule.
`n = 200,000`, 8 seeds for the full grid and 40 seeds for the reruns that carry the win
probability. Source: `experiments/data/A4/utility_curves.csv`, `utility_curves_primary.csv`,
`utility_curves_all.csv`, `utility_curves_summary.csv`, `utility_curves_smoothness.csv`.

**Two sweeps, and the difference between them is itself a finding.** A provider's delivered mean
enters the mechanism twice, once as the bid it elicits with and once as what it delivers after it
has won, and a single constant conflates them.

* `sweep = all`: deliver the swept mean in EVERY phase. This is the only sweep a router admits,
  since a router has no phases.
* `sweep = primary`: bid the capability, as the proposed profile does, and sweep only the PRIMARY
  delivery. This is where the mechanism's incentive property lives, and it has no counterpart
  under a router.

Reporting only the first would understate the mechanism and reporting only the second would not be
comparable to a router, so both are here. **The two sweeps share a delivered-mean grid, so a file
that mixes them and is sorted by delivered mean shows the two series interleaved and looks
bimodal. That is a reading hazard I created and have now removed**: the aggregate carries an
explicit `series_id`, the duplicate rows the companion job produced have been dropped (1,846 of
5,602, all of which agreed exactly with their originals), and one CSV per sweep is written.

### 4.7 The two-top condition bites at the bid-noise scale, not at gap zero

Section 2c measures that the top two capabilities are never exactly equal, and section 4.2
measures the revenue collapse that follows. But the theorem states its condition as EXACT
equality while the mechanism shades its announced price anyway, by `s_n` for the finite-context
mechanism and by `3 s_n + rho_n` for the continuum one. So the binding quantity should not be
whether the gap is zero but whether the gap is small RELATIVE TO THAT SHADING: if the runner-up
can still win the elicitation it keeps a stake in its own elicited value and the price holds up;
if it cannot win under any bid it becomes indifferent, shades, and the price collapses.

**Construction, and it is CONSTRUCTED rather than measured.** Take the measured triple
`GPT-4 | Claude-V2 | Mistral-7B` on the Q = 7 context set, keep the top and third providers
exactly as measured, and replace the runner-up's capability function by `M_top(z) - delta` at
every context, so the top-to-second gap is exactly `delta` everywhere. `delta = 0` is then the
instance in which one real model's capability function is DUPLICATED as a second provider, which
is not artificial: two vendors serving the same open-weight model, or one model behind two
regions, are tied by construction, and redundancy of that kind is common in serving. The
zero-mass measurement of section 2c stands unchanged; it is over eleven DISTINCT models, which is
the worst case for ties, and this is a separate and complementary experiment.

`delta` is swept over {0, 0.001, 0.002, 0.005, 0.0075, 0.01, 0.015, 0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2,
0.4}, which spans both candidate scales. For each `delta` the equilibrium revenue is reached by
best-response iteration over the rich option class, and the runner-up's utility-against-effort
curve and its total variation are recorded, since that flatness IS the indifference. The two
scales, at `n = 300,000` with `L_n = 10,700`:

* `s_n = 0.0494`, the price shading the finite mechanism applies;
* `sd of the bid difference = 0.0068`, which is how far apart two capabilities have to be before
  the runner-up stops winning the elicitation by luck. This is the scale I expect the transition
  to sit at, and it is about `s_n / 7`, so the two candidate scales are well separated and the
  sweep can tell them apart.

## 5. The monitor ladder, including a two-sample-test rung

One context, so the ladder isolates the monitor and nothing else. Two axes: the FALSE CONVICTION
RATE of a fully compliant primary winner, one that plays the proposed profile exactly, and the
DETECTION DELAY in primary rounds against a winner that elicits at its capability and then drops.
Two deviators: `baitswitch` drops to the intrinsic level, `baitswitch_mild` drops halfway. 300
replicates per cell, `n = 300,000`. Source: `experiments/data/A4/monitor_ladder.csv` and
`monitor_frontier.csv`.

**The two-sample rung.** Gao, Liang and Guestrin (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2410.20247) detect a distorted
served model with a Maximum Mean Discrepancy two-sample test, reporting a median 77.4 per cent
power from an average of ten samples per prompt. That is structurally the job our monitor does, so
the ladder carries a rung that decides by a batched two-sample test instead of the anytime
cumulative boundary. **It is not their test and not their system**: theirs compares full output
distributions, this one compares a batch of binary outcomes against a reference sample drawn at the
announced price. The comparison is of designs, not implementations.

A design note that is itself a finding: the obvious reference sample, the winner's own elicitation
outcomes, is WRONG here, and the ladder records it as the rung `twosample_elicit`. The mechanism
instructs the winner to drop from its bid to the announced price, so testing the served stream
against the bid convicts a fully compliant winner in **1.000** of replicates. The correct reference
is the price.

**The frontier, which is the only fair comparison.** Comparing delays without matching the false
conviction rate is meaningless, because any monitor can be made fast by making it trigger-happy.
At `alpha = 0.25`, 300 replicates:

| rung | false conviction rate | median delay, full bait and switch | median delay, mild |
|---|---|---|---|
| anytime boundary, half width | **0.000** | **48** | **176** |
| anytime boundary, the paper's width | **0.000** | **199** | 798 |
| anytime boundary, double width | **0.000** | 807 | 3,202 |
| two-sample, batch 1000, alpha 1e-6 | **0.000** | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| two-sample, batch 200, alpha 1e-6 | 0.003 | 200 | 1,000 |
| two-sample, batch 50, alpha 1e-6 | 0.023 | 150 | 7,300 |
| two-sample, batch 200, alpha 1e-4 | 0.133 | 200 | 400 |
| anytime boundary, quarter width | 0.180 | 10 | 31 |
| two-sample, batch 50, alpha 1e-4 | 0.690 | 50 | 800 |
| two-sample, batch 200, alpha 0.01 | 1.000 | 200 | 200 |
| anytime boundary with the slack term removed | 1.000 | 1 | 1 |
| two-sample against the winner's own bid | 1.000 | 200 | 200 |
| no monitor | 0.000 | never | never |

**Result, and it goes the paper's way.** At a false conviction rate of zero the paper's anytime
boundary detects a full bait and switch in a median of **199** primary rounds and the best
two-sample rung that also reaches zero needs **1,000**, a factor of five; at half width the
boundary reaches 48, a factor of twenty. My earlier reading of this ladder, before the compliant
arm had run, compared delays without matching the false conviction rate and got the opposite
answer. The matched comparison reverses it.

**Why, and it is the reason the anytime boundary exists.** A batched test cannot fire before its
batch completes, so its delay is bounded below by the batch size, and its false conviction
probability over a horizon `H` is about `1 - (1 - alpha)^(H/B)`. At `H = 300,000`, `B = 200`,
`alpha = 1e-4` that predicts 0.139 and the measurement is **0.133**, which is a direct check on the
explanation. To drive it to zero over this horizon the batch must grow, and the delay grows with
it. The cumulative boundary controls the probability of EVER convicting a compliant winner by a
union bound over all rounds, so it buys a horizon-uniform guarantee with no batch size to tune, and
it can still fire at any round. That is exactly the trade the paper claims and it survives
measurement.

**And the slack term is doing what Section 5 of the paper says.** Removing it convicts a fully
compliant winner in **1.000** of 300 replicates, against **0.000** for every anytime rung at half
width or above.

**Honest caveats.** This is one context, one instance family and `alpha = 0.25` (the `alpha = 0.5`
and `0.75` compliant arms were still running when this was written and are in the CSV when they
land). The two-sample rung is a normal-approximation z test on a batch of binary outcomes, not an
MMD test on output distributions, so the comparison is between a batched design and an anytime
design, and it should not be reported as a comparison with any published system.

## 5b. BASELINE 1: the single-observation exclusion rule, under our observation model

**What this is, and what it is not.** Esmaeili et al. (AAAI 2025, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v39i13.33510,
arXiv:2403.15115) give the private-capacity mechanism this paper extends. As our related work
describes it, the rule elicits a bid with ONE pull per agent, announces the second-highest bid as
the price, and excludes an agent whose delivery falls below it. In THEIR setting the selected agent
observes and edits its realised reward before the platform sees it, so a single pull certifies the
bid and the rule is correct. **This is that rule transplanted into this paper's noisy-delivery
model, where the agent fixes a target before a fresh Bernoulli draw. It is not a reproduction of
their results in their own setting, and nothing here is evidence that their mechanism is wrong. It
is solving a different problem, and this experiment measures why the difference matters.** It is
the measured form of Theorem `thm:separation`, which until now had a three-line proof and no
measurement.

Implementation: `run_single_observation_exclusion` in `scripts/mechanisms.py`. The elicitation
length is generalised to `L` so the transition can be seen; `L = 1` is the rule as stated. When
every agent has been excluded the platform either collects nothing further, which is the model
Theorem `thm:separation` bounds, or falls back to round robin, which is a charitable repair; both
are reported. `n = 300,000`, 30 seeds, 4 triples, 5 assumptions about `mu`, 20 instance cells,
every provider playing the proposed profile exactly. Source:
`experiments/data/A4/baseline_single_observation_summary.csv`.

**Theorem `thm:separation` is confirmed, and with a very large margin.** Under the policy the
theorem models, the rule collects between **0.0000 and 0.0119 per round** against the monitored
second price's **0.5995**, and it excludes **2.2 to 3.0 of the 3 providers** even though all three
are playing the proposed profile exactly. Its compliant-exclusion count is 2.2 to 3.0; the
monitored second price's is **0.000** in all 20 cells. The platform is idle for 73 to 100 per cent
of the horizon.

**A statement sharper than the theorem, and it is immediate.** With ONE Bernoulli pull per agent
the elicited bids are 0 or 1, so the announced price is 0 or 1 and is **never a capability**.
Either it is 0, in which case the rule asks nothing and protects nothing, or it is 1, in which case
no provider can meet it and the winner is certain to be excluded. Measured over seeds the price is
1 on **0.733** and 0 on **0.267**, and there is no third case. That is not a rate at which the rule
degrades under noise; it is that a single pull cannot express a price at all. A unit test pins it:
`test_single_pull_elicitation_can_only_announce_zero_or_one`.

**The charitable repair does not rescue it, and the exploitability shows why.** Letting the
platform fall back to round robin once everyone has been excluded restores revenue to 0.47 to 0.62,
which looks comparable to the monitored second price's 0.60. But it is comparable only because the
rule has degenerated into unprotected round robin: every provider has been excluded, so there is no
price, no monitor and no exclusion left. The best deviation found confirms it: against the repaired
rule a single deviator gains **0.047 to 0.158 per round on average and up to 0.272**, against
**0.045 on average and 0.105 at worst** for the monitored second price. Full sweep:
`experiments/data/A4/baseline_single_observation_exploit.csv`.

**What the paper should take from this.** Section 3 currently proves the separation and asserts its
consequence. It can now state it as measured: transplanted into the noisy model, the
single-observation rule retains between 0.00 and 0.02 of the per-round revenue that the monitored
second price retains on the same measured instances, and it ejects every complying provider. The
labelling above must travel with the number.

## 5c. BASELINE 2: the fixed-buffer monitor

Braverman et al. monitor a selected arm's running average against the announced price using a FIXED
concentration buffer after a burn-in. This paper's central design choice is a boundary that GROWS
with the number of pulls, and the ladder had a no-slack rung and a two-sample rung but nothing with
a fixed buffer, so the device had never been tested against the alternative that exists in the
literature. The rung sweeps the buffer over {0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20} and the burn-in
over {100, 1000, 10000, n^{3/4} = 12,819}, 24 settings, `n = 300,000`, 200 replicates,
`alpha = 0.25`. Source: `experiments/data/A4/monitor_ladder.csv` and `monitor_frontier.csv`.

**First measured fact: for a fixed buffer the detection delay IS the burn-in.** Against a winner
that drops to its intrinsic level, the median delay is 100, 1000, 10000 and 12,819 primary rounds
for the four burn-ins, and it is the same for every buffer from 0.005 to 0.2. After the burn-in the
running average of a floored deviator is already below the price by more than any of these buffers,
so the buffer does not enter; only the burn-in does.

**Second measured fact, and it is the half the ladder previously could not test: a fixed buffer of
size c cannot convict a deviator that shades by less than c.** A provider that knows the published
buffer delivers a constant amount below the announced price and never recovers from it. Conviction
rate over 200 replicates, burn-in 100:

| deviator shades by | buffer 0.005 | 0.01 | 0.02 | 0.05 | 0.10 | **0.20** | anytime boundary, paper's width |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.360 | **0.000** | **1.000**, median delay 9,915 |
| 0.10 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | **0.015** | **1.000**, median delay 2,520 |
| 0.19 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | **0.875** | **1.000**, median delay 689 |

**Third measured fact: a small buffer convicts compliant winners.** At buffer 0.005 the false
conviction rate of a fully compliant winner is 0.96 at burn-in 100, 0.78 at burn-in 1,000 and 0.34
at burn-in 10,000. (The full 24-setting compliant sweep was still running when this was written;
these three are from the completed rungs and the rest land in the same CSV.)

**The prediction holds and no setting avoids both.** A fixed buffer must be small enough not to
tolerate persistent shading and large enough not to convict noise, and those requirements move in
opposite directions as the number of pulls grows, because the noise in a running average shrinks
like `1/sqrt(r)` while a fixed buffer does not shrink at all. Measured: buffer 0.005 convicts a
compliant winner in 0.96 of replicates; buffer 0.2 never convicts a provider shading by 0.05
forever, which is worth 0.05 per round to it in perpetuity. **The anytime boundary has a false
conviction rate of 0.000 at half width and above and convicts every one of the three constant
shadings, in 689 to 9,915 primary rounds.** Its admitted shading is `b_n(r)/r`, which tends to zero,
against a fixed buffer's `c`, which does not. That is the argument the paper makes for a growing
boundary, and this is the measurement of it.

**This is not a hypothetical family: it is the monitor a competing peer-reviewed mechanism uses.**
The Second-Best Utility Guarantee Mechanism of section 5d tests a running average against a FIXED
slack `(b + p L) M / 3` after a burn-in `B = T^{2 eps}`, which is exactly this rung. At
`n = 300,000` its slack is 0.075 to 0.096 and its burn-in is 4,482. Measured, its behaviour is the
one this sweep predicts for a fixed slack of that size: it never convicts a compliant winner, it
never convicts a 10 per cent shading or an undercut either, and its detection delay against a full
floor is 4,483 rounds, which is its burn-in to within one. So the trade-off drawn here is not a
straw alternative; it is where an independently designed mechanism actually sits, and it sits at
the permissive end.

**The one thing that would have been a negative result, and was not.** If some (buffer, burn-in)
had reached a false conviction rate near zero AND a delay below the boundary's 199 rounds AND
convicted the constant shadings, the paper's device would be dominated. None does: the settings
fast enough to beat 199 rounds are the burn-in-100 ones, and at burn-in 100 the only buffers that
catch a 0.05 shading are 0.05 and below, which convict compliant winners at rates from 0.36 to
0.96.

## 5d. BASELINE 3: the Second-Best Utility Guarantee Mechanism, in two settings

**What this is, and what it is not.** Cao, Wang, Liu, Li, Tao and He, "Pay for The Second-Best
Service: A Game-Theoretic Approach Against Dishonest LLM Providers", WWW 2026, pages 249 to 260,
DOI 10.1145/3774904.3792446, arXiv:2511.00847v5. **No code and no artifact were released, so this
is a reimplementation from the paper.** It is THEIR mechanism run under THIS paper's observation
model. **Nothing here reproduces their results in their own setting, where they are correct.**
Implementation: `run_second_best` in `scripts/mechanisms.py`, following their Mechanism 1 line by
line including the exploration, exploitation and two blind-trust phases.

**The transplant, and the three modifications.** Their observation model is strictly more general
than ours, so ours is a special case and the mechanism carries over with no change to its
instrument set, its monitor, its phase budgets or its proposed provider strategy.

* **(M1)** token length `L = 1`, which disables the billing channel. Per-round payment is then the
  constant `p`, their user utility is our platform revenue minus `p`, and their benchmark is
  `n (M_(2) - p)`.
* **(M2)** reward range `R = 1`, the reward being our Bernoulli draw. The constant `b` in their
  line 16 is the reward range; the Hoeffding step in their Appendix A, which bounds
  `P[|v_bar - h(c)| >= bM/3]` by `2 exp(-2B(bM/3)^2 / b^2)`, fixes `b = R = 1`.
* **(M3)** a common price `p := min_i (M_i - mu_i)`, with their truthful cost corresponding to
  delivering capability, so a cost `c` maps to a delivered mean
  `m = mu_i + (c/p)(M_i - mu_i)`.

**Where their theorem's two assumptions hold, checked rather than asserted.** Their Theorem 5.2
assumes (i) a positive correlation between incurred cost and delivered user utility and (ii) that
as cost rises the growth in reward outpaces the increase in total cost. Under the linear map of
(M3), (i) is `dm/dc = (M_i - mu_i)/p > 0`, which always holds, and (ii) is
`(M_i - mu_i)/p >= 1`, which holds for every provider **because `p` was chosen as the minimum
effort range**, with equality at the provider attaining that minimum. So on the equal-effort-range
linear-cost sub-family both assumptions hold with equality throughout, and off it they hold
strictly. What we do NOT inherit is their discrete-action correction and their billing channel,
both of which (M1) removes.

**Generosity, as instructed, and the two ambiguities.** `epsilon = 1/3`, which balances their
`O(T^{1-eps} log T + T^{2eps})` and is their optimum.

1. Their `T_R` formula reserves more budget than the two blind-trust phases consume and the paper
   does not say what to do with the remainder. **Resolved in their favour**: the leftover is
   delegated to the winner rather than left unserved.
2. Their line 21 is `if validated`, and whether it gates blind trust phase II as well as phase I is
   not typographically resolved in the pseudocode. The primary runs take the reading on which the
   line-18 break means the remaining queries are never delegated, which is the reading the brief
   specifies and the one on which their guarantee is coherent. **Because that reading is the less
   generous one, the alternative is measured too** rather than argued about, as a full parallel
   sweep (job 64879828, `gate_blind2 = 0`). It matters: on the highest-infeasibility contextual
   triple with a bait-and-switch winner, revenue per round is **0.0321** under the gated reading
   with 94.0 per cent of the horizon undelegated, and **0.1858** under the generous reading with
   68.9 per cent undelegated. Both are reported and both are far below what a mechanism that
   reallocates obtains.

**The scoring, and the accident that makes it clean.** With `L = 1` their proposed provider
behaviour reduces to delivering the smallest feasible mean meeting the announced value, which is
our own proposed profile. So both mechanisms can be scored under IDENTICAL PROVIDER BEHAVIOUR and
the whole difference reduces to elicitation budget, monitor design and failure handling. Their
phases map onto ours as exploration to elicitation, exploitation to our priced primary phase, and
blind trust to our fallback. **That mapping is generous to them on revenue**, because our proposed
profile delivers capability in the fallback while their own Theorem 5.2 has providers deliver the
minimum in blind trust, so both scorings are reported: `proposed` is identical behaviour and
`their_equilibrium` is their own.

### Setting two, contextual: the cost of pricing a task-dependent capability with a scalar rule

**Their mechanism was never claimed for contextual settings and is correct in the setting it does
claim.** It is run here deliberately outside its stated scope, and the question is NOT whose
mechanism is better: it is what it costs to price a task-dependent capability with a scalar rule.
**Our own scalar mechanism sits in the same column for exactly that reason**, so the finding is
about scalarness rather than about any one design, and our contextual mechanism is the third
column. Triples are selected by the ANALYTIC mass on which one announced price exceeds the
winner's capability, computed before any of this ran: the minimum
(`Claude-Instant-V1 | Claude-V1 | GPT-4`, 0.0000), the median
(`CodeLlama-34B | Mixtral-8x7B | WizardLM-13B`, 0.2266) and the maximum
(`Claude-V1 | Llama-70B | WizardLM-13B`, 0.4527).

Measured, 6 cells: their announced price stands above the winner's capability on **0.2544 of
arrival mass**, close to the 0.2266 the analytic calculation predicts for the median triple, and
revenue is **0.6072 under identical behaviour** and **0.4664 under their own equilibrium** against
a second capability of 0.5999. So under their own equilibrium the shortfall against the second
capability is **0.133 per round**, and it is a shortfall a scalar rule cannot avoid on this data
because the object it prices does not exist: there is no single capability to price.

**The honest reading, and it is a claim about the problem rather than about their paper.** A scalar
rule meets a task-dependent capability in one of two ways. Ours announces a price the winner cannot
always meet and convicts it, which we measure at 0.270 for the pooled variant. Theirs announces a
price with a slack wide enough to absorb the variation, which never convicts but also never
enforces anything finer than 0.086. **Neither prices the task, and the two failure modes are
different in kind**, which is exactly why having two independently designed scalar mechanisms in
the same column is worth more than having one.

**The finding, and it is about the problem rather than about anyone's paper.** Two independently

## 6. Findings that complicate or damage the paper's claims

Collected here so they are not buried.

**6a. The continuum mechanism's oscillation allowance is vacuous at every reachable horizon.**
Section 4.4 sets `rho_n = sqrt(D) h_n log(en)` with `h_n` the region side. At `n = 300,000`,
`d = D = 2` and `h_n = 1/13` this is `rho_n = 1.481`, with `s_n = 0.265`, so the total price
shading `3 s_n + rho_n = 2.277` and the announced price
`tau_C = (beta_(2),C - 3 s_n - rho_n)_+` is **identically zero**: the mechanism offers no
protection at all. The full arithmetic is `experiments/data/A4/rho_audit.csv`. The total shading
is still 1.359 at `n = 10^7`, 0.665 at `n = 10^9`, and only reaches 0.212 at `n = 10^12`, against
elicited values around 0.75. At `d = 1` it is 0.991 at `n = 300,000` and 0.188 at `n = 10^9`. This
is arithmetic on the paper's own constants, not a simulation. It does not contradict the theorem, which is asymptotic, but the paper currently
says the allowance "is doing the work it was introduced to do", and at any horizon a reader
could run, it is doing the opposite: it is switching the mechanism off. I ran the continuum
configurations twice, once with the paper's `rho_n` and once with `rho` set to the 95th
percentile of the MEASURED within-cell oscillation of the capability surface (0.20 at d = 2,
k = 200). **That second value is a changed design constant and it is labelled as such
everywhere.** Both versions are reported.

**6b. The profile-local mechanism cannot complete elicitation on a real context distribution.**
RouterBench's benchmark families are very unequal: MMLU carries 0.404 of the arrival mass and
MT-Bench 0.0023. A local elicitation block needs `K L_n` visits inside its own context, and
`L_n` grows like `n^{2/3}`, so a context of mass `p` completes only when
`p n >= K n^{2/3}(log en)^{1/3}`. For MT-Bench that requires `n` of order `10^10`. At
`n = 300,000` the Q = 7 runs leave the small contexts permanently in elicitation. This is the
theorem's own `Q` threshold, and Section 5 already reports it on synthetic instances, but the
real context distribution makes it bite far harder than a uniform one: the binding quantity is
not `Q` but the SMALLEST CONTEXT MASS, and the paper does not say so. The headline finite runs
are therefore reported on three context sets: Q = 7 with real masses (where it bites), Q = 7
with uniform masses, and Q = 3 restricted to the three families with adequate mass.

**6c. The premise is weak at the top of this model set.** GPT-4 is the pointwise best model on
six of the seven contexts and its context-averaged capability (0.813) is 0.115 above the next
model. Only two models are ever pointwise best. A reviewer who reads the paper's motivating
claim as "no single provider is best at everything" will find that RouterBench, in 2024, is
close to a counterexample at the top, and that the contextual structure the paper needs lives in
the SECOND capability and in the pairwise ordering below the leader. The paper should make that
distinction rather than assert the stronger version.

**6d. The flip rate itself depends on the evaluation protocol.** 0.3013 on the 5-shot table
against 0.1749 on the 0-shot table, from the same eleven models on the same benchmarks. Quote
the range.

**6e. At the proposed profile, the fallback pays a mechanism for failing, so revenue does not
discriminate.** This is the most consequential thing I found and it is a property of the model,
not of my code. In `S*` every non-winner delivers its capability during elicitation AND during
the fallback. When the monitor fires, the mechanism restarts EXP3 over a set of providers all of
whom are delivering `M_i`, so fallback revenue converges to the LARGEST capability, which is
strictly above the second capability the theorem promises. A mechanism whose price is infeasible
therefore ejects its own compliant winner into a phase that earns MORE than compliance would
have. That is exactly what happens to the scalar mechanism on this data: at `n = 3,000,000` it
convicts a compliant winner in 0.523 runs on average, and its measured revenue ends up 0.0226 per
round ABOVE the contextual mechanism's. Three consequences the paper should absorb.

* The revenue guarantee `P(n;S*) >= E sum M_(2)(X_t) - o(n)` is a lower bound that a broken
  mechanism can beat. Revenue at the proposed profile is therefore not a valid comparator between
  two mechanisms in this model, and Section 5 should say so where it compares rungs on revenue.
* The quantity that does separate them is the one the continuum argument is built on: whether the
  announced price is feasible at the realised winner at every context of the region being priced.
  I measure it directly and the separation is total (0.000 against 0.523 compliant convictions;
  0 per cent against 19.6 per cent of arrival mass with the price above capability).
* If a reviewer runs the obvious experiment, they will find the scalar mechanism earning more.
  The paper is much better off stating this first, with the explanation, than being shown it.

**6f. The two-top condition holds on exactly zero arrival mass.** The condition that at least two
providers attain the largest capability is what rules out the low-revenue equilibria, what makes
the public contextual equilibrium work, and what governs pairwise collusion. It is an exact-tie
condition. On the measured capability table it is satisfied on **0.0000** of arrival mass, for
every one of the 55 pairs, 165 triples and 330 four-model subsets, on both context sets. Relaxed
to a tolerance of 0.01 it covers 0.056 of mass on average for triples and no subset reaches half
its mass. With all eleven models the mean gap between the top and second capability is 0.055
(`experiments/data/A4/two_top_summary.csv`). The paper should stop treating the two-top condition
as a mild regularity assumption. On real LLM capabilities it never holds, so the operative
guarantee is always the honest benchmark, and the second-capability revenue the paper leads with
is unreachable. The constructive reading is that the paper should say what an approximate
two-top condition buys: the natural conjecture, which I did not prove and did not test, is that a
capability gap of `g` at the top degrades the equilibrium revenue by something like `g` rather
than collapsing it, and 0.055 would then be a tolerable loss rather than a total one.

**6g. Why the router does not degrade, and why that is a property of the utility model.** In the
paper's model a selection is worth exactly 1 to the selected provider and the effort it costs is
`m - mu <= 1`, so every selection is worth a non-negative amount even at full effort. A rule that
routes to the estimated best provider therefore makes shading self-defeating: a provider that
shades enough to be overtaken loses selections worth 1 each to save at most 1 each, and loses the
race. That is why the best response to a quality-estimating router in the declared stationary
class is to deliver capability, and it is essentially the paper's own public-capability theorem
(Theorem `thm:grid-ucb-robust` and Theorem `thm:contextual-public`) showing up in simulation. Two
things follow. First, the paper cannot motivate its mechanism by claiming that quality-estimating
routing collapses under strategic providers; in this model it does not. Second, the case where
routing genuinely fails is the one the paper already identifies: a UNIQUELY top provider can
undercut to just above the runner-up, stay selected, and bank the difference. That is the
motivation the paper should lead with, it is exactly the two-top condition, and section 4.4
measures what it costs.

## 7. What this does not show

Specific to this package, not boilerplate.

1. **It shows nothing about any real provider's conduct.** No source I used documents deliberate
   quality degradation by any provider. The strategic layer is a simulator. A reader must not
   come away thinking that any of these eleven models, or the companies behind them, shaded
   quality, and the paper must not phrase it in a way that permits that reading.
2. **It does not measure `mu_i`, and cannot.** RouterBench records one configuration per model.
   Every statement about effort, exploitability, revenue relative to the honest benchmark, and
   the equilibrium profile is conditional on the assumed `alpha`. A different assumption about
   what a provider delivers at minimum effort moves every one of those numbers.
3. **It does not show that the contextual mechanism earns more revenue than the scalar one at a
   deployable horizon.** At `n = 300,000` it does not, on 29 per cent of the 165 model subsets,
   and the correlation with the flip rate that the analytic calculation predicts is absent from
   the simulated revenue. What it shows is a price-feasibility difference.
4. **It is not an evaluation of any published routing system.** The router baseline is a policy
   class I wrote: per-context empirical means plus greedy, epsilon-greedy or UCB selection. I did
   not train, run, tune or reproduce FrugalGPT, RouteLLM, Hybrid LLM, Zooter, AutoMix or
   RouterBench's own routers, and no number here should be attributed to any of them. Those
   papers are cited as the literature the policy class stands for, nothing more.
5. **It is not a reproduction of either reimplemented mechanism in its own setting.** The
   single-observation exclusion rule of Esmaeili et al. and the Second-Best Utility Guarantee
   Mechanism of Cao et al. are both **reimplemented from their papers**, neither of which released
   code or an artifact, and both are run under THIS paper's observation model. Both are correct in
   the settings they claim. For the second-best mechanism in particular: it was never claimed for
   contextual settings, and the contextual column exists to measure what it costs to price a
   task-dependent capability with a scalar rule, with OUR OWN scalar mechanism in the same column
   so that the finding is about scalarness and not about any one design. Two choices in its
   pseudocode are ambiguous and are documented in section 5d; one is resolved in their favour and
   the other is measured both ways rather than argued. If any sentence anywhere reads as a claim
   about the quality of either paper's contribution, it is mislabelled and should be rewritten.

6. **It is not a reimplementation of model equality testing.** The two-sample monitor rung is a
   batched two-sample test on a stream of binary outcomes against the announced price. Gao,
   Liang and Guestrin (ICLR 2025, arXiv:2410.20247) test whether an API is serving a distorted
   model using a Maximum Mean Discrepancy two-sample test on full output distributions, and
   report a median 77.4 per cent power from an average of ten samples per prompt. Different
   statistic, different data, different null. The rung is included because it does the same JOB,
   and the comparison is of designs, not of implementations.
7. **The continuous contexts are as good as the embedding, and the embedding is cheap.** TF-IDF
   plus LSA at d = 2 explains 8.8 per cent of the term-matrix variance. It recovers the benchmark
   family well (purity 0.891) but it is not a semantic embedding, and a better one would very
   likely raise the measured continuous flip rate. The continuous numbers are a floor, not an
   estimate.
8. **The exploitability numbers are lower bounds.** They are the best gain over a declared,
   finite option set of eight per agent, not a supremum over the behavioural class. Section 5 of
   the paper computes exact suprema by backward induction for the profile-local mechanism; this
   package does not, and its numbers must not be quoted as exploitability in the theorem's sense.
   Where they are quoted they are called "the best deviation found in the declared set".
9. **The strategic world is a best response inside a stationary class.** Providers here choose
   one option and keep it. The paper's private results cover arbitrary nonanticipating
   behaviour; this simulation does not, and a phase-dependent deviation could do better.
10. **Bernoulli rewards are an idealisation on part of this data.** MMLU, HellaSwag, Winogrande,
   ARC and MBPP scores are binary 0/1. GSM8K scores are graded (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 dominate) and
   MT-Bench scores are normalised ratings. Taking `M_i(z)` to be the mean score is faithful; the
   Bernoulli draw at the chosen target is the paper's model, not a resampling of the data.
11. **One embedding, one seed for the SVD, one triple-selection rule.** The three model triples
    were stratified by measured flip rate (maximum, median, minimum, ties broken
    lexicographically) before any mechanism was run, but they are three of 165.

## 8. On a head-to-head against the contract line, which I did not run

As instructed, no head-to-head against Saig et al. (NeurIPS 2024) or Sreenivas and Larson
(arXiv:2608.18232) was attempted. For the record, the reason a shared axis is hard is sharper
than "different settings". Those mechanisms buy quality with MONETARY TRANSFERS; ours buys it
with ALLOCATION. The only common objective I can construct is principal surplus, quality
obtained minus what the principal gave up, and to write that down one needs an exchange rate
between one allocation slot and one unit of currency. In our model a selection is worth exactly
1 to the provider and costs the platform nothing beyond a round it was going to spend anyway,
so the platform's budget is `n`, which is not money. In a contract model the budget is money the
platform would otherwise keep. The exchange rate that reconciles them is a free parameter, and
whatever number it is set to is what decides which side wins. Any head-to-head would therefore
report the assumed exchange rate rather than a property of the mechanisms. If the paper wants to
position against that line, the honest move is the one it already makes in related work: state
the instrument (transfers against allocation) and the information assumption, and not tabulate.

## 11. Implementation notes worth recording

**A real overflow hazard in the paper's own constants.** The price radius is
`s_n = sqrt(log(2 K n^4) / (2 L_n))` and the boundary uses `log(2 n^4)`. For `n >= 2^16` the
quantity `n^4` exceeds the range of a 64-bit integer, and a straightforward implementation either
overflows or silently falls back to arbitrary-precision objects and then fails inside the
logarithm. The tests caught this at `n = 50,000`. Every logarithm in the engine is expanded as
`log(a) + 4 log(n)` in floating point. Anyone implementing Sections 3 or 4 at the horizons the
paper discusses will hit this, and one sentence in the appendix would save them the hour.

**Pickle safety.** RouterBench publishes Python pickles, which execute arbitrary code on load.
They are loaded through a restricted unpickler (`scripts/safe_load.py`) that refuses every global
outside numpy, pandas and a short allowlist. Both files load cleanly under it.

**The tests were written before the analysis**, as required, and cover the four minima the brief
names (phase accounting, feasibility, reward-sum integrality, and the collapse of the contextual
mechanism onto the scalar one at a single context) plus the price construction, the oscillation
allowance, the benchmark ordering and the design constants. 46 tests, all passing.
